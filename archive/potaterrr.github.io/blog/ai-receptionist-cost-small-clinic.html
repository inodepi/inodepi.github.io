What an AI receptionist actually costs a small clinic

Nov 8, 2026 / 4 min read / 787 words / Christian John
VoiceOpsGuardrails
On this page

    Nobody in this space publishes a price, and the reason is boring: the same agent can cost one clinic very little and another quite a lot, and not because of the AI. It's call volume, call length and how many systems the booking has to touch. So instead of a fake fixed number, here's the honest cost map — the three layers, what moves each one, and the questions that make two quotes comparable.

    Layer 1: voice minutes

    This is the part people remember and the part least in your control. A voice platform bills per minute of conversation, and the rate depends on the engine, the voice, and whether transcription and call analysis are bundled. A typical small clinic might see a few hundred minutes a month; a busy one several thousand.

    What moves it: average call length (a booking is shorter than a caller describing symptoms), how much the agent talks (a chatty greeting costs minutes), and how many calls it answers versus hands to a human quickly.

    Rough shape, not a quote: voice minutes are usually the largest recurring line on a low-volume clinic, because per-minute rates sit in the cents-to-low-dimes range and volume is still real.

    Layer 2: the automation layer

    Someone has to turn the call into a booked slot and an alert. That lives in n8n (my default), Make, Zapier or a small Python service. Costs split into hosting and operations:

    • Self-hosted n8n on a small VPS is often the cheapest predictable option — a flat monthly fee regardless of how many executions run.
    • Make or Zapier charge by operations or tasks, which is fine at low volume and gets expensive as calls and steps multiply.
    • A small Python service has the lowest marginal cost but needs a home and someone who can maintain it.

    What moves it: how many steps a booking takes (calendar check, conflict retry, logging, alert), and whether the client wants to own the account or hand it over.

    Layer 3: the one-time build

    This is the scoped project: voice agent configuration, the booking pipeline, intake fields, guardrails, error branches, the test calls, documentation and handover. It scales with the number of appointment types, the number of systems to touch, and how much the clinic wants the agent to decide on its own.

    A single bookable service with one calendar is a small build. Five services, two locations, insurance questions and a review gate is a larger one. That's why "how much for an AI receptionist?" gets a scope question back — the range between those two builds is wide, and anyone quoting before asking is guessing.

    A worked example, with the variables left in

    Say a clinic misses 120 calls a month and the agent averages three minutes per answered call. That's ~360 voice minutes. Add a VPS for n8n, a phone number, and a one-time build. The monthly part is small and predictable; the build is the real number. Now change one variable — calls averaging six minutes because patients describe their week — and voice costs double while the build doesn't move at all.

    That's the whole cost story: recurring scales with talk time, one-time scales with scope. Treat any vendor who quotes a monthly figure without asking your call length as a reseller of surprises.

    What should be in every quote

    1. Voice platform and rate per minute, including transcription and analysis.
    2. Automation hosting and who owns the account.
    3. One-time build scope, revision rounds, and what happens when the process changes.
    4. Who pays for the calendar, phone number and any LLM usage.
    5. Support: what's included after handover, and the hourly rate if it isn't.

    If a quote omits lines 1–4, it's not comparable to another quote. Put both side by side on those five items and the cheapest option is usually obvious — and it's rarely the lowest monthly number.

    Does it pay for itself?

    Do the clinic's own math, not the vendor's. Take the value of one booked appointment, multiply by calls currently missed per month, and apply a conservative capture rate — not every missed call becomes a booking. If that number is bigger than voice minutes plus the retainer, it works. If the clinic misses three calls a month, it likely doesn't, and a better voicemail is the honest recommendation.

    The short version

    Voice minutes scale with talk time. Hosting scales with platform choice. The build scales with scope. There is no honest fixed price, and a good quote makes all three visible instead of rolling them into one number that hides the trade-offs.
